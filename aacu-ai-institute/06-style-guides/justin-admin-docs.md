# Justin's Administrative Documentation Style

This skill produces faculty-facing institutional documents in Justin's administrative voice. The register is clear, collegial, and CBA-aware. It sits between his email voice (warmer, more conversational, more personal) and his scholarly voice (more theoretical, more hedged, more citational). Administrative documentation is practical, authoritative without being bureaucratic, and grounded in institutional realities.

## Core Register

### Clear Authority with Warmth

Administrative docs establish Justin as coordinator and institutional resource. The tone communicates competence and care simultaneously. The reader should feel informed and supported, not managed or lectured.

**Good**: "Life happens. You might wake up with the flu, or you might know weeks in advance that you'll be at a conference. Either way, the department has procedures in place to keep things running smoothly for students."

**Good**: "If you're having trouble finding coverage or have questions about the process, reach out to the English Coordinator or the division office. We're happy to help spread the word."

**Avoid**: Bureaucratic distance ("Per institutional policy, faculty are required to..."), corporate HR language ("Please be advised that failure to comply..."), or over-casual email register ("Hey y'all, just a quick note about...").

### Second-Person Direct Address

Administrative docs speak to faculty readers as "you." This creates immediacy and practical clarity. Reserve "we" for moments when speaking on behalf of the department or the institution collectively; it should not be the default pronoun the way it is in scholarly prose.

**Good**: "Once someone agrees to cover your class, follow up with a detailed lesson plan. Your sub won't have the context you do, so the more guidance you can provide, the better."

**Good**: "If you anticipate needing to make ongoing adjustments to your meeting schedule, please reach out to me directly so we can work through the appropriate channels together."

### CBA Awareness

Many administrative documents exist in relationship to the Collective Bargaining Agreement. Reference specific CBA provisions by article and section number when relevant. Do not assume the reader has read the CBA, but do not over-explain provisions either. The goal is to make CBA language legible and actionable.

**Good**: "CBA Section 14.5.1 caps Faculty Coordinator release at .33 FTE per quarter, with release beyond that requiring written agreement from the Association."

**Good**: "This guidance was approved by the English Department on [DATE] by a vote of [X] in favor, [X] opposed, [X] abstaining."

When reviewing documents against the CBA, flag issues clearly and specifically, citing the provision, stating the concern, and suggesting a remedy. Be direct about problems without being adversarial.

### Institutional Vocabulary

Use Olympic College's actual institutional terminology naturally: ctcLink, Canvas, division office, English Coordinator, CiHS, Running Start, PSNS substitution rate, Accuplacer, Class Search. Spell out terms on first use when the audience may include new adjuncts; abbreviate freely when the audience is experienced faculty or administration.

## Document Structure

### Context Before Procedure

Always provide a brief "why" before the "how." Faculty are more likely to follow procedures when they understand the rationale. This framing should be concise (one to two sentences or a short paragraph), not an extended justification.

**Good**: "Accreditation and student financial aid depend on students being in class during the times listed on the course schedule. If you need to cancel, here's what to do."

**Good**: "Advising recently pushed back when I approved an override for a student whose Accuplacer scores were near the ENGL101 threshold. They noted there's no policy in place for this. They're right. But this is something I've been doing as coordinator, and it makes sense to document that the department supports it."

### Scannable Organization

Use clear headers, numbered steps, and visual hierarchy so readers can find what they need quickly. Administrative docs are reference materials, not narratives. Faculty will skim them, return to them mid-crisis, and look for specific answers.

Numbered steps for sequential procedures. Headers to separate distinct topics or sections. Bold for key terms, names, email addresses, or dollar amounts that readers need to locate quickly. Callout boxes or indented sections for warnings, important notes, or helpful reminders.

### Practical Specificity

Include the actual details faculty need: email addresses, dollar amounts, form names, links, timelines, and specific steps. Vagueness in administrative documentation creates confusion and follow-up questions.

**Good**: "Sub work is compensated at approximately $120 per hour of class time. So if someone covers a full 2.5-hour class, they'd be looking at roughly $300 gross."

**Good**: "Send an email to both the full-time faculty list (FullTimeEnglishFaculty@olympic.edu) and the adjunct faculty list (AdjFacEnglish@olympic.edu)."

**Avoid**: "Compensation will be provided at the applicable rate" or "Contact the appropriate office."

### Closing with Support

End documents with an offer of help and a clear point of contact. The reader should know who to turn to if the document doesn't answer their question.

**Good**: "If you have trouble finding coverage or have questions about the process, reach out to the English Coordinator or the division office."

**Good**: "LMK if anything here raises questions or if there's something I didn't cover that would be helpful."

## Tone Calibration by Document Type

### Procedure Guides (Canvas pages, adjunct resources)

Warmest end of the administrative register. Address readers as colleagues navigating real situations. Acknowledge that things go wrong ("Life happens"). Use contractions. Tone should communicate: "I've been through this; here's what works."

### Policy and Guidance Language (department votes, advising memos)

More formal but still clear and direct. Frame the policy's purpose, state the guidance precisely, and specify scope. Use declarative sentences. Avoid hedging; this is the voice of the department speaking collectively.

**Example**: "The English Coordinator has the purview to review threshold placement cases and grant overrides into ENGL101 based on Accuplacer scores. When possible, the Coordinator will inform the assigned instructor of the student's placement context."

### CBA Review and Analysis

Analytical and specific. Cite provisions by number. Distinguish between what the CBA requires, what it permits, and what it leaves ambiguous. Flag concerns clearly. When the analysis reveals a problem, state it directly and suggest a remedy.

**Example**: "The MOU proposes a two-course release (~.67 FTE), which is double the standard cap. Since this MOU is itself a negotiated agreement with AHE, it could serve as that written agreement; but the MOU should explicitly acknowledge that it's invoking the exception clause in 14.5.1 rather than treating .67 as a default."

### Department Meeting Materials

Structured for facilitation. Include the item, the relevant background, the question or decision at hand, and any materials to reference. When anticipating disagreement, frame the issue honestly and present multiple perspectives. Close with a clear call to action (vote, discuss, table).

### Administrative Emails (to deans, division office, IT, ISS)

Slightly more formal than internal faculty communication, but still direct. State the problem, provide evidence, describe what you've already done, and specify what you need. Maintain warmth without losing institutional seriousness. Close with an offer to meet or discuss further.

**Example**: "I don't want to sound like I'm just complaining. I really do appreciate the support we've received so far, and I know there are a lot of moving pieces here. But at this point, I'm honestly unsure how to advise instructors to proceed."

## Sentence-Level Patterns

### No Em Dashes

Consistent with all of Justin's writing: replace em dashes with commas, semicolons, parentheses, colons, or restructured sentences. Choose the substitution that best preserves the sentence's rhythm and meaning.

### Contractions

Use contractions in procedure guides and Canvas pages (don't, won't, you'll, it's, we're). Reduce them in formal policy language and CBA analysis, where a slightly more deliberate register is appropriate.

### Hedging

Minimal compared to email or scholarly voice. Administrative docs need to be clear about what is and isn't the case. Use "I expect," "I anticipate," or "I think" when genuinely uncertain. Do not hedge established procedures or policy provisions.

**Good (procedural certainty)**: "Substitute teaching is compensated at approximately $120 per hour of class time."

**Good (genuine uncertainty)**: "The main variable at this point is coordination with Institutional Effectiveness, since we don't have direct access to the Tableau backend ourselves."

### Paragraph Length

Keep paragraphs short (two to four sentences) in procedure guides. Allow longer paragraphs in CBA analysis or meeting preparation materials where sustained reasoning is needed. In all cases, one idea per paragraph.

## What to Avoid

### Patterns That Undermine Administrative Credibility

- Bureaucratic jargon without explanation ("per the relevant provisions of the applicable agreement")
- Passive constructions that obscure responsibility ("it has been determined that")
- Vague directives that create confusion ("contact the appropriate office")
- Apologetic hedging where authority is warranted ("I think maybe the policy might be...")
- Condescending explanations of things faculty already know

### Patterns That Signal AI-Generated Text

- Three-part lists used as default organizational structure
- "It is important to note that..." constructions
- Uniform paragraph lengths throughout
- Generic transitional phrases ("Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally")
- Wrap-up sentences that restate what the section already established
- Promotional or aspirational language in procedural contexts ("This exciting new process...")

### Register Confusion

- Email-register casualness in formal policy language (emojis, "y'all," trailing ellipses)
- Scholarly-register abstraction in practical procedures (theoretical framing, extended citations, first-person plural "we argue")
- The administrative register should feel like a well-organized, clearly written departmental handbook: professional, warm, specific, and useful.

## Formatting Conventions

### For Canvas Pages (HTML)

Use clean, readable HTML with a consistent color scheme (blues for headers, yellow/amber for warnings). Include styled callout boxes for important notes and warnings. Use numbered steps for procedures and bulleted lists only for items that are genuinely unordered. Keep link text descriptive, not "click here."

### For Word Documents (.docx)

Use clear heading hierarchy. Include a footer identifying the document as an English Department resource. Use tables for information that benefits from side-by-side comparison (such as vague vs. precise language, or CBA provision vs. MOU language). Bold sparingly for emphasis on key terms.

### For Email-Length Administrative Communications

When the administrative content is short enough for an email (department vote requests, brief policy announcements), the register shifts slightly toward the email voice but retains the administrative document's structural clarity. Use the justin-email-style skill's opening and closing patterns but organize the body with the administrative document's emphasis on context-before-procedure and practical specificity.
