## Olympic College's five Core Abilities

1. Communication
2. Thinking
3. Information Literacy and Technology
4. Lifelong Learning
5. Global Perspective

Search results describe these as taught across programs and disciplines, so that every student
works toward them regardless of area of concentration — which is exactly the property that makes
them the right foundation for a definition that has to work in every discipline.

---

## The method

For each Core Ability, three questions:

1. **What does AI change** about how students demonstrate this ability?
2. **What must be protected** as human work, because the ability itself is what we claim to
   develop?
3. **What does AI make newly necessary** for a student to be able to do?

The assembled answers are the definition of AI literacy. The answers to question 2, gathered up,
are the values statement — the human capacities we are choosing to keep at the center. Note that
they are not invented for the occasion; the college named them already.

**And the faculty-facing version:** AI literacy for Olympic College faculty is the understanding
required to keep delivering these five abilities to students in an environment where AI can
convincingly imitate their products.

---

## 1. Communication

**What AI changes.** Fluent, well-organized prose is now cheap to produce, which means the
polish of a communicative product no longer evidences communicative competence. Drafting,
formatting, translation, and register-shifting can all be delegated. The gap between a student
who can write and a student who can obtain writing has narrowed to near invisibility on the page.

**What must be protected.** The capacity to form a position and stand behind it. Audience
judgment — knowing what *this* reader, patient, client, or crew needs to hear. Accountability for
what one has said. Live, unscripted communication: speaking, listening, responding, negotiating
disagreement in real time. These are the parts of the ability that were always the point.

**What AI makes newly necessary.** Directing and evaluating AI-generated communication rather
than accepting it. Recognizing when AI output is fluent but empty, or has flattened a voice, or
has said something the student does not actually mean. Disclosing AI involvement honestly.
Knowing which communicative acts should not be delegated at all — a condolence, a disciplinary
conversation, a patient explanation.

## 2. Thinking

**What AI changes.** The visible *product* of reasoning — the analysis, the worked problem, the
argument — can now be generated without the reasoning happening. This is the ability most
threatened by AI, because the evidence we have always accepted for it is precisely what AI is
best at imitating.

**What must be protected.** The productive struggle through which reasoning capacity is actually
built; there is no version of this ability that can be acquired by watching. Judgment about what
counts as a good answer, which is what lets a person evaluate anything, AI output included.
Metacognition — knowing what one does and does not understand.

**What AI makes newly necessary.** Evaluating AI reasoning for soundness rather than for
confidence. Recognizing confabulation, and knowing that fluency and correctness are unrelated in
these systems. Understanding characteristic failure modes well enough to anticipate them.
Knowing which problems require human judgment because the stakes, the values, or the
accountability cannot be delegated.

## 3. Information Literacy and Technology

**What AI changes.** The most directly affected of the five. AI is now a primary information
interface for many students, and it synthesizes without citing, asserts without sourcing, and
fabricates references that look correct. The retrieval model that information literacy was built
around — find a source, evaluate the source — does not describe what a generative system does.

**What must be protected.** Source evaluation and the understanding of provenance: where a claim
came from, who made it, and why that matters. The ability to find and read a primary source
rather than a summary of a summary.

**What AI makes newly necessary.** Understanding that a generative model produces plausible text
rather than retrieving verified fact — the single most consequential thing a student can
understand about these tools. Verifying claims against actual sources. Recognizing that training
data carries bias and omission, and asking whose knowledge is represented. Data privacy: what
happens to what you type in. Choosing the right tool, and knowing when the answer is none.

## 4. Lifelong Learning

**What AI changes.** Cuts both ways, more than any other ability here. AI can short-circuit the
learning process, and dependency can quietly erode the capacity to learn without it. It is also a
genuinely powerful learning aid — a patient explainer, an infinite source of practice problems,
an always-available tutor for a student who cannot get to office hours. Our students, many of
whom work and commute, may benefit from that more than traditional students would.

**What must be protected.** The capacity to learn something difficult without a shortcut, and
the tolerance for the discomfort that requires. Self-direction. The honest self-assessment that
tells you whether you have actually learned something or merely obtained it.

**What AI makes newly necessary.** Using AI to learn rather than to avoid learning — a
distinction students are rarely taught to make and faculty rarely model. Noticing when reliance
is eroding one's own capability. Adapting to tools that will change repeatedly across a working
life, which is a lifelong-learning problem more than a technology problem.

## 5. Global Perspective

**What AI changes.** AI increasingly mediates how students encounter people and places unlike
their own, and these models encode particular cultural and linguistic perspectives while
presenting output as neutral. Translation barriers have fallen dramatically, which is a real
gain. The labor and environmental costs of these systems are distributed globally and unevenly.

**What must be protected.** Genuine engagement with difference rather than a summary of it.
Cultural humility. The recognition that a mediated account is not an encounter.

**What AI makes newly necessary.** Recognizing whose perspectives are represented in AI output
and whose are absent or flattened. Understanding that "the AI said" carries an unmarked point of
view. Awareness of who builds these systems, who labels their data, and who bears their
environmental costs. Attention to unequal access — globally, and among our own students.

## Cross-cutting

Three things run through all five rather than sitting under any one, and probably belong in the
definition's framing:

- **Disclosure and honesty** about AI use, which is an integrity question but also a
  communication and thinking question.
- **Judgment about delegation** — deciding what to hand to a machine is itself a skill, and it is
  the one students are least often taught.
- **Purpose.** Every question above ultimately reduces to: what is this activity *for*? An
  assignment whose purpose is a product can be automated; one whose purpose is a capacity cannot.

## For the workshop

Reading the abilities this way will surface a real disagreement, and it is better surfaced than
avoided: **which of these abilities must students still hold themselves, and which might the
college reasonably decide are now routinely performed with AI assistance?** Faculty will not
agree, and the disagreement will fall differently across disciplines. Making it discussable in
the specific terms of our own stated commitments is more useful than settling it by default,
course by course.
