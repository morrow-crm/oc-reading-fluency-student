# AI Literacy Definition — Method and Working Template

The definition of AI literacy for Olympic College faculty will be built from the college's
**new Core Abilities**, which are in development and not yet published. This file holds the
method and the template; the substance gets filled in once the abilities are available.

**Why the Core Abilities are the right foundation.** They are what the college says every
student should be able to do on leaving, regardless of program — which means they are the one
framework every discipline already answers to. A definition anchored there works in a welding lab
and a composition classroom by construction rather than by good intentions. And we are not asking
anyone to adopt a new framework; we are asking what the college's own commitments require now
that AI can perform parts of the work those abilities describe.

**Timing works in our favor.** Faculty will be learning and discussing the new Core Abilities
this year regardless. The question *what does AI change about our ability to deliver these?* can
be part of introducing them rather than a separate initiative competing for attention.

---

## The method

For each Core Ability, ask three questions:

1. **What does AI change** about how students demonstrate this ability?
2. **What must be protected** as human work, because the ability itself is what we claim to
   develop?
3. **What does AI make newly necessary** for a student to be able to do?

The assembled answers are the **definition of AI literacy**. The answers to question 2, gathered
up, are the **values statement** — the human capacities we are choosing to keep at the center.
They are not invented for the occasion; the college named them.

**The faculty-facing version:** AI literacy for Olympic College faculty is the understanding
required to keep delivering these abilities to students in an environment where AI can
convincingly imitate their products.

## Patterns we expect to hold

Provisional, from a first pass against the college's previously published abilities. Useful as
starting hypotheses; discard any that don't fit the new framing.

- **Whichever ability concerns reasoning and analysis will be the most threatened** — not because
  students are more tempted there, but because the evidence we have always accepted for it (the
  analysis, the worked problem, the argument) is precisely what AI is best at imitating. This
  points at assessment design rather than detection.
- **Anything built on an information-retrieval model needs rewriting, not extending.** "Find a
  source, evaluate the source" does not describe what a generative system does. That a model
  produces plausible text rather than retrieving verified fact is the single most consequential
  thing a student can understand about these tools.
- **Learning-related abilities cut both ways.** AI can short-circuit learning and erode the
  capacity to learn unaided. It is also a patient tutor available at 11pm to a student who works
  days and cannot reach office hours — which for our students may matter more than it would
  elsewhere. The plan should not flatten this into a threat.
- **Communication's real content survives better than it appears.** AI takes the polish. It does
  not take forming a position and standing behind it, audience judgment, or live unscripted
  exchange — which were always the point.

## Cross-cutting themes

Three run through every ability rather than sitting under one, and probably belong in the
definition's framing:

- **Disclosure and honesty** about AI use — an integrity question, but also a communication and
  reasoning one.
- **Judgment about delegation.** Deciding what to hand to a machine is itself a skill, and the one
  students are least often taught.
- **Purpose.** Every question above reduces to: what is this activity *for*? An assignment whose
  purpose is a product can be automated; one whose purpose is a capacity cannot.

## For the workshop

This exercise will surface a real disagreement, better surfaced than avoided: **which abilities
must students still hold themselves, and which might the college reasonably decide are now
routinely performed with AI assistance?** Faculty will not agree, and the disagreement will fall
differently across disciplines. Making it discussable in the specific terms of our own stated
commitments beats settling it by default, course by course.

## Open questions

- **When are the new Core Abilities published, and can the team see the draft before then?** Step
  2 depends on them, so publication timing is a real dependency on the fall timeline.
- Who is developing them — and would they welcome the AI question being raised as part of the
  rollout rather than after it?
- Will there be outcomes-assessment data against the new abilities, or a baseline from the old
  ones worth carrying forward?
- Which body owns them? That is our endorsement route for the definition.
